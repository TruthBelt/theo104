1 · Saved by Grace
ReadEtzel and Small, Everyday Theology
  • 26. What Does Salvation Look Like to You? pp. 179–183
Opens in Logos.

Soteriology (from the Greek sōtēria, “deliverance” or “salvation”) is the doctrine of how God rescues sinners (Etzel and Gutierrez, 139). Module 5 left us at the mirror, looking at a person of dignity, marred by sin, and unable to cure himself. Only through God’s intervention can humanity solve “its greatest problem, which is alienation from God” (Etzel and Small, 177). This module is the turning point of the course, where the bad news meets the good news.

More Than a Moment

Everyone has a vision of salvation, because everyone has some idea of what is wrong with the world: political, social, or personal, from new laws to “a new job, a relocation, or a new circle of friends” (Etzel and Small, 179). Christianity names the problem clearly and offers a clear solution. Many think of salvation as the single moment they believed; other traditions stress growth and the sacraments. Both belong to the picture: “Salvation is something that God has done, is doing and, ultimately will do!” It is “at once something the Christian possesses by faith, realizes in a deeper way moment-by-moment through obedience, and awaits in the future” (Etzel and Small, 180).

Scripture speaks of salvation on three levels: national (Israel delivered from Egypt, Exod. 14:13), personal (Ps. 51:12), and global (Isa. 65:17). In the New Testament, Jesus offers salvation to individuals (Mark 5:34; John 20:31), and Revelation shows believing Israel saved and the world renewed (Etzel and Small, 180–81).

An Order of Salvation

Paul lays out the sequence in one chain: “Those whom He foreknew He also predestined … and those whom He predestined He also called, and those whom He called He also justified, and those whom He justified He also glorified” (Rom. 8:29–30). Theologians call this the order of salvation (Latin ordo salutis), an order that is “logical (not necessarily always chronological)” (Etzel and Small, 181). Each term names one facet of a single rescue:

StageWhat It NamesWhen
ElectionGod’s choice to save, made before creationEternity past
CallingGod’s summons through the gospelAt conversion
RegenerationThe Spirit’s gift of new birthAt conversion
ConversionOur response of repentance and faithAt conversion
JustificationGod’s declaration that we are righteousAt conversion
AdoptionOur welcome into God’s familyAt conversion
SanctificationThe lifelong process of becoming holyThe Christian life
PerseveranceGod’s keeping of His people to the endThe Christian life
GlorificationThe full and final completion of salvationEternity future

Beneath them all runs union with Christ (Deck 6).

It’s All About Grace

Salvation rests entirely on grace, defined in the first century as “a superior showing of undeserved favor to an inferior,” as when a king chooses to be kind to a servant he owes nothing (Etzel and Small, 182). That is what God has done in Christ: “For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast” (Eph. 2:8–9). Grace “is not too difficult for most to grasp intellectually, but it is hard to grasp emotionally,” because we live in a world of conditional acceptance (Etzel and Small, 182). Think of the person who has shown you the most grace: “God is far more gracious to us than this person.” Christian salvation “is not simply a definition to be understood, but a relationship with a gracious God to be realized” (Etzel and Small, 182–83).

2 · Chosen in Christ
ReadEtzel and Small, Everyday Theology
  • 27. When Does Salvation Begin? pp. 185–189
Opens in Logos.

Election is God’s choice, made before the creation of the world, to save a people for Himself. Salvation does not begin when we first believe: “before the world began … salvation was already conceived in the mind of God” (Eph. 1:4; Etzel and Small, 185). Three biblical terms describe it: predestination, to set one’s destiny ahead of time; foreknowledge, to know ahead of an event; and election, to choose (Etzel and Small, 185). God knows the end from the beginning because He has planned it: “My counsel shall stand, and I will accomplish all My purpose” (Isa. 46:10).

Election in Both Testaments

In the Old Testament, God chose Abraham, so that “in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed” (Gen. 12:3), and the nation of Israel, “not because you were more in number than any other people” but because He loved them (Deut. 7:7–8). Israel’s privileges, the covenants, the law, the temple, and the promised Messiah, did not mean the personal salvation of every Israelite (Rom. 9:1–13; Etzel and Small, 185–86). In the New Testament, election is bound up in Christ: God “chose us in Him before the foundation of the world, that we should be holy and blameless before Him” (Eph. 1:4). Paul’s first words about it are “Blessed be”: election moved him to praise and comforted him, because God “works all things according to the counsel of His will” (Eph. 1:11; Rom. 8:28; Etzel and Small, 186–87).

The Debate

“The debate about election in Christian theology is not whether election is biblical … but rather concerns the basis upon which God elects” (Etzel and Small, 187):

ViewThe Claim
Unconditional electionGod chooses people for salvation without considering what they have done or will do
Conditional electionGod chooses on some basis in the person, usually because He foresees that the person will choose Him

Scripture points toward unconditional election. Of Jacob and Esau, “though they were not yet born and had done nothing either good or bad—in order that God’s purpose of election might continue, not because of works but because of Him who calls—she was told, ‘The older will serve the younger’ ” (Rom. 9:11–12). God chooses in a manner “not tied to human activity” (Etzel and Small, 187).

Election and Our Choices

Election troubles many Christians, but Scripture never presents it to trouble them (Etzel and Small, 188). It does not make our choices any less our own. Our freedom is real, though not absolute, and “we still make decisions for which we can and will be held justly morally accountable” (Etzel and Small, 188). God’s plan does not cancel our choices; it includes them as the very means He uses: “Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling, for it is God who works in you, both to will and to work for His good pleasure” (Phil. 2:12–13).

Why it matters: Election assures us that salvation is primarily a work of God. “He who began a good work in you will bring it to completion at the day of Jesus Christ” (Phil. 1:6). In a world that feels out of control, election “should not be a source of trouble, but one of profound comfort and joy!” (Etzel and Small, 188–89).

Before conversion comes conviction, an awareness of sin and of our need for God’s offer of salvation, and that awareness comes through the gospel call (Etzel and Small, 189). A general call goes out to all whenever the gospel is preached, and it is offered sincerely to everyone; the call that brings a person to faith is the one Paul means when he writes, “Those whom He called He also justified” (Rom. 8:30). “Many are called, but few are chosen” (Matt. 22:14). The point is the same as election’s: “God, not us, moves first in our salvation” (Etzel and Small, 189).

Can the Dead Respond?

“The natural person does not accept the things of the Spirit of God, for they are folly to him, and he is not able to understand them” (1 Cor. 2:14). “Apart from the intervention of the Holy Spirit, a person is unable to even understand their need for God” (Etzel and Small, 189). Theologians answer this in two ways (Etzel and Small, 189–90):

ViewThe ClaimAssessment
Prevenient graceGod gives all people a grace that cancels original sin, so that all can freely respondUnlikely: “There are no biblical texts that clearly affirm” it
The Spirit’s effective offerThe Holy Spirit personally offers salvation to persons and brings them to receive itThe textbook’s view

Both agree that God must step in first. Even hearing His voice is a gift of His grace, and refusing it is no small matter: “Upon each rejection of God’s voice, our spiritual sensitivity is lessened” (Heb. 3:15; Etzel and Small, 190). The right answer is Samuel’s: “Speak, for Your servant hears” (1 Sam. 3:10).

Conversion: Repentance and Faith

Conversion is “our willing response to the gospel call, in which we sincerely repent of sins and place our trust in Christ for salvation” (Grudem, quoted in Etzel and Gutierrez, 139). Conversion stories vary widely, but every genuine one rests on “two closely related but distinguishable acts” (Etzel and Small, 191).

  • Repentance. In the Old Testament, repentance is turning from sin and returning to the Lord: “Return to Me, and I will return to you” (Mal. 3:7). In the New Testament, the Greek metanoia means “a change of mind,” not mere remorse but going in a new direction (Etzel and Gutierrez, 139–40). Paul sets godly grief, which “produces a repentance that leads to salvation,” against worldly grief (2 Cor. 7:10). True repentance engages the whole person: “one’s intellect is aware of sin, one’s emotions experience sorrow over sin, and one’s behavior turns from sinful actions,” and it continues through the Christian life (Etzel and Small, 192).
  • Faith. Saving faith rests on the person and work of Jesus: “These are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God” (John 20:31; Rom. 10:9–10). The Greek pistis means a conviction about the truth of something, and faith itself is God’s gift (Eph. 2:8–10): “God provides us with the faith to believe in Him” (Etzel and Gutierrez, 140). Genuine initial faith “will continue to grow into faithfulness,” for “faith by itself, if it does not have works, is dead” (Jas. 2:17; Etzel and Small, 193).

Conversion involves the whole person, intellect, emotion, and will: “Salvation includes all of these components—through the work of Jesus and the new life provided by the Holy Spirit” (Etzel and Gutierrez, 139). And fruit is the result of salvation, not its cause: “One is justified by faith apart from works of the law” (Rom. 3:28; Etzel and Gutierrez, 140).

Regeneration “is an inner re-creating of fallen human nature by the gracious sovereign action of the Holy Spirit” (Evangelical Dictionary of Theological Terms, quoted in Etzel and Gutierrez, 117), the “spiritual birth brought forth by the Holy Spirit at conversion” (Etzel and Small, 194). Conversion is salvation from our side; regeneration is salvation from God’s side. Conversion is “a conscious act of repentance toward God and faith in Jesus”; regeneration is “an unconscious transformation brought about by the Holy Spirit” (Etzel and Small, 194; Etzel and Gutierrez, 117–18).

Life First

“Theologians debate whether regeneration occurs prior to or simultaneously with conversion” (Etzel and Small, 194). The two happen together in time. But in order, life comes first, because the dead must be made alive before they can respond. God, “even when we were dead in our trespasses, made us alive together with Christ” (Eph. 2:5), and “everyone who believes that Jesus is the Christ has been born of God” (1 John 5:1).

Born Again

Jesus told Nicodemus, “Unless one is born again he cannot see the kingdom of God” (John 3:3). Nicodemus thought of a physical rebirth; Jesus meant a spiritual one. “The wind blows where it wishes … So it is with everyone who is born of the Spirit” (John 3:8): we do not see the wind, only its effects (Etzel and Small, 194–95). Only God can give this life. The prophets promised Israel a new heart and the Spirit’s life (Ezek. 36:26; 37:14), promises God will keep to Israel; the same Spirit gives new birth now (John 3:5; Etzel and Gutierrez, 117).

The word itself is rare. The Greek paliggenesia, “new life” or “renewal,” appears only in Titus 3:5 and Matt. 19:28 (Etzel and Gutierrez, 118). God “saved us, not because of works done by us in righteousness, but according to His own mercy, by the washing of regeneration and renewal of the Holy Spirit” (Titus 3:5). Yet the idea fills Scripture: re-creation (2 Cor. 5:17), resurrection (Col. 2:13), circumcision of the heart (Deut. 10:16; Col. 2:11), washing (1 Cor. 6:11), and new birth (John 1:13). Each shows the Spirit doing an inner work that creates new desires: “Humans are unable to change their desires and identity apart from divine intervention,” and “the product of all true regeneration is new desires directed to follow God’s ways” (Etzel and Small, 195).

Not Rejuvenation

At the Last Supper Jesus told Peter, “The one who has bathed does not need to wash, except for his feet” (John 13:10). Regeneration is the bath, given once; daily confession of sin is the washing of the feet that keeps our fellowship with Him (Etzel and Gutierrez, 118). God “didn’t just resuscitate our sinful souls; He washed our souls clean and gave us a new life” (Etzel and Gutierrez, 119). We still sin, but “sin will have no dominion over you, since you are not under law but under grace” (Rom. 6:14). A gift this great should make us grateful in word, thought, and deed (Etzel and Gutierrez, 119).

5 · Declared Righteous

  • 24. Propitiation pp. 133–136

Justification is “an instantaneous legal act of God in which he (1) thinks of our sins as forgiven and Christ’s righteousness as belonging to us, and (2) declares us to be righteous in his sight” (Grudem, quoted in Etzel and Gutierrez, 123). People try to ease their guilt through good causes and charity, but biblical guilt is objective: “whether we feel guilty or not we stand guilty before God” (Rom. 3:9–20; Etzel and Small, 197). God’s answer is grace: all “are justified by His grace as a gift, through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus” (Rom. 3:23–24).

Declared Righteous

Justification is a legal term, picturing a courtroom: the sinner stands before the Judge, awaiting a verdict (Etzel and Small, 198). The Greek dikaiosynē indicates a right position with God. The slogan “just-as-if-I’d-never-sinned” is easy to remember, “but unfortunately it is not an accurate representation of the biblical meaning” (Etzel and Gutierrez, 123). A court can find a defendant “not guilty” who is not innocent, and we are not innocent (Etzel and Gutierrez, 123). Yet God does more than acquit. He counts Christ’s righteousness as ours (Phil. 3:9): justification is “an act of God’s free grace, wherein He pardoneth all our sins, and accepteth us as righteous in His sight, only for the righteousness of Christ imputed to us, and received by faith alone” (Westminster Shorter Catechism, quoted in Etzel and Gutierrez, 123). It happens “in an instant” (Etzel and Gutierrez, 124), is never repeated or increased, and brings peace with God (Rom. 5:1).

Two Words at the Heart of the Cross

  • Redemption means “to buy back.” Sin had become our slave master; in grace, God buys us back and reunites us with our Creator (Etzel and Small, 198). The price was Christ’s death, “a ransom for many” (Mark 10:45), paid to God’s justice, not to Satan.
  • Propitiation is “an offering that turns away the wrath of God directed against sin” (Pocket Dictionary of Theological Terms, quoted in Etzel and Gutierrez, 133); the Greek hilasmos means “an appeasing” (Etzel and Gutierrez, 134). God’s wrath is revealed against sin (Rom. 1:18), and “God is not able to merely overlook our sin” (Etzel and Small, 198). Yet “He loved us and sent His Son to be the propitiation for our sins” (1 John 4:10).

At the cross “the judgment of God and the love of God converge”: “For our sake He made Him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in Him we might become the righteousness of God” (2 Cor. 5:21; Etzel and Small, 198). Our sin was counted to Him, though He did not become a sinner. “The beauty of the cross is that the one who stands as our judge is the very one who has taken our judgment and offers to us justification” (Etzel and Small, 199).

The Reformation Debate

Justification was the central dispute of the sixteenth-century Reformation (Etzel and Small, 199):

PositionJustification IsChrist’s Righteousness Is
Historic ProtestantInstantaneous and forensicImputed, all at once
Roman CatholicProgressiveImparted gradually through the sacraments

Progressive justification “seems to minimize the once-for-all significance of Jesus’s death” (Etzel and Small, 199). Because our acceptance before God rests on Jesus’s work, not our own, we can “live without fear of failure” (Etzel and Small, 199), and our later sins do not discharge us from God’s family (Etzel and Gutierrez, 124).

Adoption is God’s welcome of believers into His family as His children. “It is one thing to have right standing before God in a judicial sense; it is another level of intimacy to be welcomed into God’s family through adoption” (Etzel and Small, 200). “To all who did receive Him, who believed in His name, He gave the right to become children of God” (John 1:12). God is our Father, who provides for His children (Matt. 6:25–34) and disciplines them for their good (Heb. 12:7–11); Jesus is our brother, and all Christians are brothers and sisters. We cry “Abba! Father!” (Gal. 4:6–7), and we are “heirs of God and fellow heirs with Christ” (Rom. 8:17; Etzel and Small, 200).

Sanctification

Sanctification is “a progressive work of God and man that makes us more and more free from sin and like Christ in our actual lives” (Grudem, quoted in Etzel and Gutierrez, 127). To be sanctified is to be set apart: for God, and from sin. The Hebrew qadowsh and the Greek hagios both mean holy, set apart (Lev. 20:26; Etzel and Gutierrez, 127): “As He who called you is holy, you also be holy in all your conduct” (1 Pet. 1:15). Scripture speaks of it in three tenses (Etzel and Small, 200; Etzel and Gutierrez, 128):

TenseMeaningScripture
PositionalSet apart to God at conversion“You were sanctified” (1 Cor. 6:11)
ProgressiveBecoming holier in character and conduct“Being transformed … from one degree of glory to another” (2 Cor. 3:18)
FutureEntirely set apart to God in gloryHe “will transform our lowly body” (Phil. 3:21)

The Spirit and the Disciplines

“Holiness is not something that we achieve through mere personal effort. Holiness is produced as we allow the Holy Spirit to control our thoughts and lives. There is no holiness without the Holy Spirit” (Etzel and Small, 200–201). Christians divide over whether holiness begins as an event or a process (Etzel and Small, 201):

ViewHoliness BeginsReading of Acts
PentecostalAs an event: a baptism of the Spirit after conversionThe normal pattern for every believer
GradualAs a process of keeping in step with the SpiritPentecost as a once-for-all moment in God’s plan

Because the Spirit has come, every believer now has Him (1 Cor. 12:13), and the call is to “keep in step with the Spirit” (Gal. 5:25). Life-changing moments after conversion are real, but they are better named “a deepening understanding of the person and power of the Holy Spirit” than a second blessing, and “for those who have not experienced a singular transformative moment, do not be disheartened” (Etzel and Small, 201–2).

Sanctification advances as we order our lives around growth. The spiritual disciplines—Bible study and meditation, fasting and prayer, service, confession, and corporate worship—are vehicles through which the Spirit works, but they “are lifeless without the Spirit!” (Etzel and Small, 202). Scripture is the test of all guidance and “the foundation to all spiritual maturity.” Do not try to become a spiritual superhero overnight; aim to “become stronger today than you were yesterday” (Etzel and Gutierrez, 129–30).

Union with Christ

Beneath every stage of salvation runs one reality: union with Christ. Believers are chosen in Christ (Eph. 1:4), made alive with Him (Eph. 2:4–5), justified in Him (1 Cor. 1:30), sanctified as they abide in Him (John 15:4–5), and will be glorified with Him (Col. 3:4). “All aspects of Christian salvation could be ordered under the larger topic of union with Christ” (Etzel and Small, 203). Jesus pictures it as a branch abiding in the vine: “Whoever abides in Me and I in him, he it is that bears much fruit, for apart from Me you can do nothing” (John 15:5). “The Christian is not merely to learn the words of Jesus. The Christian is actually to get to know Jesus,” in the church as well as alone (Etzel and Small, 203).

Opens in Logos.

Salvation calls for endurance to the end. Paul grew more passionate over time, knowing that “salvation is nearer to us now than when we first believed” (Rom. 13:11). The Christian is to “fight the good fight of faith” (1 Tim. 6:12) and finish the race (Etzel and Small, 205).

Can a Christian Lose Salvation?

“It appears that salvation is primarily a work of God rather than a work of human will” (Etzel and Small, 206), and what God begins, He finishes. The Father (Rom. 8:28–30), the Son (“no one will snatch them out of My hand,” John 10:28), and the Spirit, “the guarantee of our inheritance” (Eph. 1:13–14), all secure it (Etzel and Small, 206). What of those who profess faith and then fall away? Some are genuine believers passing through a season of struggle; others never truly believed. “They went out from us, but they were not of us; for if they had been of us, they would have continued with us” (1 John 2:19). Leaving is not salvation lost, but salvation “never initially possessed” (Etzel and Small, 206).

Living with Assurance

The goal is full assurance of salvation. John wrote “to you who believe in the name of the Son of God, that you may know that you have eternal life” (1 John 5:13). Assurance rests on God’s promise to the one who believes in His Son, and obedience confirms it: “By this we know that we have come to know Him, if we keep His commandments” (1 John 2:3). The textbook puts it this way: a Christian “possesses assurance of their salvation through current obedience to Christ.” Obedience is the evidence by which we know, seen in fellowship with other believers, keeping God’s commands, loving one another, and not loving the world; what we rest on is God’s promise. “Faithful obedience is the result of genuine initial faith” (Etzel and Small, 207). “Once saved, always saved” “does not diminish the Christian’s responsibility of current obedience” (Etzel and Small, 207); we strive to the end with passion, confident that God will finish what He began.

Glorification

Glorification is the final completion of salvation, after Christ’s return: “Salvation professed will ultimately become salvation fully possessed” (Etzel and Small, 207). It brings three great realities (Etzel and Small, 207):

  • The resurrection of the body. As Jesus rose, believers will be raised: “through Jesus, God will bring with Him those who have fallen asleep” (1 Thess. 4:14). Body and soul will be redeemed.
  • A redeemed community. The glorified will live together in fellowship with one another and with God.
  • A new heaven and a new earth. God will place His people in a renewed creation (Rev. 21:1), where for the first time life is lived as God intended.

“All living, this side of eternity, is a mere shadow of the fullness of life awaiting every Christian” (Etzel and Small, 207).

Why This Matters

Salvation Is God’s Work from First to Last

From election in eternity past to glorification in eternity future, every link in the chain (Rom. 8:29–30) rests on God’s initiative and grace. We truly repent and believe, but God moves first, gives life, keeps His people, and guarantees the end. This is the deepest comfort of the Christian life: “He who began a good work in you will bring it to completion” (Phil. 1:6).

Each Term Names One Facet of a Single Rescue

Election, calling, regeneration, conversion, justification, adoption, sanctification, perseverance, and glorification are not nine separate salvations but nine views of one gracious work, best gathered under union with Christ. To understand them is not to memorize a list but to see the many-sided beauty of what God has done.

The Cross Is the Center

Justification, redemption, and propitiation all meet at Calvary, where God’s holiness and God’s love meet. Because Christ bore our judgment, God is “just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus” (Rom. 3:26).

Grace Calls for a Response

Salvation cannot be earned, only received. The right response to so great a salvation is gratitude, obedience, and worship, echoing Paul: “Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed us in Christ with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places” (Eph. 1:3).

Deck 1: Saved by Grace

  1. What is soteriology?

    Show answer

    Answer: The doctrine of how God rescues sinners. From the Greek sōtēria, “deliverance” or “salvation” (Etzel and Gutierrez, p. 139).

  2. How was grace understood in the first century?

    Show answer

    Answer: A superior showing of undeserved favor to an inferior. Like a king choosing to be kind to a servant he owed nothing. That is what God has done in Jesus Christ (Etzel and Small, p. 182).

  3. Why is grace hard for many people to receive?

    Show answer

    Answer: It is easy to grasp intellectually but hard to grasp emotionally, in a world of conditional acceptance. God has shown us favor knowing full well who we are (Etzel and Small, p. 182).

  4. According to the textbook, what is Christian salvation?

    Show answer

    Answer: A relationship with a gracious God to be realized. “Christian salvation is not simply a definition to be understood, but a relationship with a gracious God to be realized” (Etzel and Small, p. 183).

Deck 2: Chosen in Christ

  1. Why did God choose Israel?

    Show answer

    Answer: He loved them and kept the oath He swore to their fathers. “It was not because you were more in number than any other people … but it is because the Lord loves you and is keeping the oath that He swore to your fathers” (Deut. 7:7–8).

  2. What does God’s choice of Jacob over Esau show?

    Show answer

    Answer: God chose before either was born or had done anything good or bad. “Not because of works but because of Him who calls” (Rom. 9:11). God chooses in a manner “not tied to human activity” (Etzel and Small, p. 187).

  3. Does election make our choices any less our own?

    Show answer

    Answer: No; God’s plan includes our choices as the means He uses, and we are accountable for them. “Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling, for it is God who works in you, both to will and to work for His good pleasure” (Phil. 2:12–13). “We still make decisions for which we can and will be held justly morally accountable” (Etzel and Small, p. 188).

  4. How did Paul respond to the doctrine of election?

    Show answer

    Answer: With praise. He opens with “Blessed be”: the language of praise. Election “should not be a source of trouble, but one of profound comfort and joy!” (Etzel and Small, pp. 186, 189).

Deck 3: Called and Converted

  1. What does “many are called, but few are chosen” show about the gospel call?

    Show answer

    Answer: The gospel goes out to many, but not all who hear are brought to faith. The general call goes out to all whenever the gospel is preached; the call that brings a person to faith is the one Paul means: “Those whom He called He also justified” (Matt. 22:14; Rom. 8:30).

  2. Why does the textbook judge prevenient grace “unlikely”?

    Show answer

    Answer: Because no biblical text clearly affirms a grace given to all that cancels original sin. “There are no biblical texts that clearly affirm a type of grace given to all that nullifies the effects of original sin.” Instead, the Holy Spirit offers salvation to persons and brings them to receive it (Etzel and Small, p. 190).

  3. How is true repentance different from feeling sorry?

    Show answer

    Answer: It is a change of mind that turns in a new direction, not mere remorse. Paul sets godly grief, which “produces a repentance that leads to salvation,” against worldly grief (2 Cor. 7:10). The Greek metanoia means “a change of mind” (Etzel and Gutierrez, pp. 139–40).

  4. What does James mean when he says faith without works is dead?

    Show answer

    Answer: Genuine faith grows into faithfulness, and works show the faith is real. “Faith by itself, if it does not have works, is dead” (Jas. 2:17). Fruit is the result of salvation, not its cause: “one is justified by faith apart from works of the law” (Rom. 3:28).

Deck 4: Born Again

  1. How do conversion and regeneration differ?

    Show answer

    Answer: Conversion is our conscious turning in repentance and faith; regeneration is the Spirit’s unconscious work of new life. “While conversion is a conscious act of repentance toward God and faith in Jesus, regeneration is an unconscious transformation brought about by the Holy Spirit” (Etzel and Small, p. 194).

  2. Conversion and regeneration happen together. Which comes first in order?

    Show answer

    Answer: Regeneration: God gives life, and the one made alive believes. God, “even when we were dead in our trespasses, made us alive together with Christ” (Eph. 2:5), and “everyone who believes that Jesus is the Christ has been born of God” (1 John 5:1).

  3. To whom did God first promise a new heart and His Spirit in Ezekiel 36–37?

    Show answer

    Answer: Israel. “I will put My Spirit within you, and you shall live” (Ezek. 37:14). These are promises God will keep to Israel; the same Spirit gives new birth now (John 3:5).

  4. What did Jesus picture by the bath and the washing of feet?

    Show answer

    Answer: Regeneration washes us once, completely; daily confession keeps our fellowship with Him. “The one who has bathed does not need to wash, except for his feet, but is completely clean” (John 13:10; Etzel and Gutierrez, p. 118).

Deck 5: Declared Righteous

  1. According to the Bible, is guilt before God only a feeling?

    Show answer

    Answer: No; whether we feel guilty or not, we stand guilty before God. “Our feeling of guilt can be subjective … The Bible says that whether we feel guilty or not we stand guilty before God” (Rom. 3:9–20; Etzel and Small, p. 197).

  2. In justification, what does God count as belonging to the believer?

    Show answer

    Answer: Christ’s righteousness. God “accepteth us as righteous in His sight, only for the righteousness of Christ imputed to us, and received by faith alone” (Westminster Shorter Catechism, quoted in Etzel and Gutierrez, p. 123; Phil. 3:9).

  3. Where do God’s judgment and God’s love meet?

    Show answer

    Answer: At the cross, where the Judge takes our judgment. “For our sake He made Him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in Him we might become the righteousness of God” (2 Cor. 5:21). “The beauty of the cross is that the one who stands as our judge is the very one who has taken our judgment” (Etzel and Small, p. 199).

  4. How does the historic Protestant view of justification differ from the Catholic view?

    Show answer

    Answer: Protestants say justification is an instant declaration, with Christ’s righteousness imputed; Catholics say it is progressive, with righteousness imparted through the sacraments. Progressive justification “seems to minimize the once-for-all significance of Jesus’s death” (Etzel and Small, p. 199).

Deck 6: Adopted and Made Holy

  1. What does adoption add to justification?

    Show answer

    Answer: A higher level of intimacy: welcome into God’s family, not only right standing. “It is one thing to have right standing before God in a judicial sense; it is another level of intimacy to be welcomed into God’s family through adoption” (Etzel and Small, p. 200).

  2. Who is the agent of holiness in the believer?

    Show answer

    Answer: The Holy Spirit. “Holiness is not something that we achieve through mere personal effort … There is no holiness without the Holy Spirit” (Etzel and Small, pp. 200–201).

  3. What are the spiritual disciplines without the Spirit?

    Show answer

    Answer: Lifeless. Bible study, prayer, fasting, service, confession, and worship are vehicles through which the Spirit works, but “the spiritual disciplines are lifeless without the Spirit!” (Etzel and Small, p. 202).

  4. What does union with Christ describe?

    Show answer

    Answer: The reality beneath every stage of salvation: chosen, made alive, justified, sanctified, and glorified in Him. “All aspects of Christian salvation could be ordered under the larger topic of union with Christ” (Etzel and Small, p. 203; Eph. 1:4; Col. 3:4).

Deck 7: Kept to the End

  1. Why did Paul grow more passionate as his life went on?

    Show answer

    Answer: He knew salvation was nearer than when he first believed. “For salvation is nearer to us now than when we first believed” (Rom. 13:11; Etzel and Small, p. 205).

  2. According to the textbook, who secures the believer’s salvation to the end?

    Show answer

    Answer: The Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. The Father glorifies those He justified (Rom. 8:30); “no one will snatch them out of My hand” (John 10:28); the Spirit is “the guarantee of our inheritance” (Eph. 1:13–14; Etzel and Small, p. 206).

  3. What does “once saved, always saved” not do, according to the textbook?

    Show answer

    Answer: Lessen the Christian’s responsibility to obey. “A belief in the phrase ‘once saved, always saved’ does not diminish the Christian’s responsibility of current obedience” (Etzel and Small, p. 207).

  4. What will glorification bring?

    Show answer

    Answer: The resurrection of the body, a redeemed community, and a new heaven and new earth. “Salvation professed will ultimately become salvation fully possessed”: body and soul redeemed, the redeemed living together with God in a renewed creation (1 Thess. 4:14; Rev. 21:1; Etzel and Small, p. 207).
