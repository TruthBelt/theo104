1 · Who Is Jesus?
ReadEtzel and Small, Everyday Theology
  • 11. Who Do You Say That I Am? pp. 79–86
Opens in Logos.

Christology is the branch of systematic theology that studies the person and work of Jesus Christ, the divine Son. Module 2 asked who the God is who has revealed Himself. This module narrows that question to its sharpest point: Who is Jesus, and what has He done?

The Center of the Faith

Christology is the center of the Christian faith, not one doctrine among many. Jesus claimed to be “the way, and the truth, and the life” (John 14:6), so a person’s understanding of Jesus shapes everything else: salvation, purpose, and eternity. As Etzel and Small put it, “The point is that Jesus is the point” (Etzel and Small, 84).

Two Halves

  • The person of Christ: His deity, His humanity, and the union of the two in one person.
  • The work of Christ: what He accomplished in His death (the atonement) and in His resurrection, and the full arc of His work from eternity past to eternity future.

The Question

The whole module unfolds from one question Jesus asked His disciples in the district of Caesarea Philippi, a region known for the worship of Baal, the Greek god Pan, and the Roman Caesar: “But who do you say that I am?” (Matt. 16:15; Etzel and Small, 79).

2 · Fully God
ReadEtzel and Small, Everyday Theology
  • 11. Who Do You Say That I Am? pp. 79–86
Opens in Logos.

The deity of Christ is the doctrine that Jesus is fully God: eternal, uncreated, sharing the very nature of God the Father. “He always has been, and always will be divine” (Etzel and Small, 84). The case rests on four lines of testimony.

Jesus Claimed It

Jesus claimed equality with the Father: “I and the Father are one” (John 10:30), after which the Jews picked up stones to stone Him (John 10:31). “Whoever has seen Me has seen the Father” (John 14:9). Most pointedly, He applied to Himself the name God gave Moses at the burning bush, “I am who I am” (Exod. 3:14). His “I am” sayings echo that name, and the plainest is “before Abraham was, I am” (John 8:58). To say “I am” in that sense is to claim to be God (Etzel and Small, 80).

His Friends Confessed It

Peter confessed, “You are the Christ, the Son of the living God” (Matt. 16:16). Thomas, after the resurrection, called Him “My Lord and my God!” (John 20:28). Paul wrote that “by Him all things were created” and that “in Him all things hold together” (Col. 1:16–17). Creating and sustaining the universe are works of God alone, and Paul credits them to Jesus.

His Enemies Understood It

When Jesus claimed to be the “I am,” the religious leaders took up stones, because they heard the claim as blasphemy (John 8:58–59). Their formal charge confirms it: “He ought to die because He has made Himself the Son of God” (John 19:7). “Jesus was killed because He said He was God” (Etzel and Small, 82).

His Actions Confirmed It

  • Miracles, called “signs” in John’s Gospel, point to His deity: water into wine, the feeding of the five thousand, walking on water, the raising of Lazarus. He also forgave sins, which the scribes rightly said only God can do (Mark 2:5–7).
  • The resurrection is the final authentication: “If Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile and you are still in your sins” (1 Cor. 15:17).

Competing Views

ViewClaim about Jesus
IslamA great prophet, but not God.
JudaismA good teacher at best, a false prophet at worst; not God.
Jehovah’s WitnessesDivine, but a created, lesser god; not to be worshiped; the Trinity denied.
Latter-day SaintsGod, but not eternally so: Jesus became a god, and humans may do the same.

(Etzel and Small, 84)

Liar, Lunatic, or Lord

Because Jesus claimed deity so plainly, “a good teacher but not God” is not an option He left open. C. S. Lewis pressed the point:

“A man who was merely a man and said the sort of things Jesus said would not be a great moral teacher. He would either be a lunatic … or else he would be the Devil of Hell. You must make your choice. Either this man was, and is, the Son of God: or else a madman or something worse.” (Lewis, Mere Christianity, quoted in Etzel and Small, 85)
3 · Fully Human
ReadEtzel and Small, Everyday Theology
  • 12. Was Jesus Really Human? pp. 87–92
Etzel and Gutierrez, Theology Applied
  • 19. Virgin Birth pp. 105–107
Opens in Logos.

The same Jesus who is fully God is also fully human: “The Word became flesh and dwelt among us” (John 1:14). The incarnation is the period that began when the Second Person of the Trinity came to earth in human flesh, including His birth, development, ministry, death, resurrection, and ongoing ministry (Etzel and Small, 87). In it the eternal Son added a complete human nature to His divine nature. He gave up nothing of His deity, and He remains man forever.

Incarnation, Not Theophany

The incarnation must not be confused with the theophanies (or Christophanies) of the Old Testament: temporary, visible appearances of God, such as the angel of the Lord who appeared to Gideon (Judg. 6:11–22). Those were brief. The incarnation began when Mary conceived (Matt. 1:18), continued through the ascension (Acts 1:9), and continues today (Etzel and Small, 88).

The Virgin Birth

The virgin birth is the doctrine that the Holy Spirit, without a human father, conceived Jesus in the womb of Mary. It fulfills Isaiah’s prophecy, “Behold, the virgin shall conceive and bear a son” (Isa. 7:14; Matt. 1:22–23). When Gabriel announced the birth, Mary asked, “How will this be, since I am a virgin?” (Luke 1:34). The doctrine carries three theological weights (Etzel and Gutierrez, 105–106):

  1. It testifies to the supernatural power of God and the unique nature of Jesus.
  2. It establishes His humanity: He was really born and “really became one of us.”
  3. It helps explain His sinlessness: “in Him there is no sin” (1 John 3:5; 2 Cor. 5:21). The angel tied the child’s holiness to the Spirit’s work (Luke 1:35).

The Evidence of His Humanity

  • Birth: He entered the world as a humble baby, not a conquering king.
  • Childhood: “Jesus increased in wisdom and in stature and in favor with God and man” (Luke 2:52). The Creator and Sustainer of the universe had to learn to read, write, and build (Etzel and Small, 88).
  • Earthly life: He was hungry (Mark 11:12), thirsty (John 19:28), and tired (John 4:6); He was tempted (Matt. 4:1–11), wept (John 11:35), slept (Mark 4:38), relied on the Holy Spirit (Matt. 4:1), submitted to the Father (Luke 22:42), and died (Luke 23:46).

“What more evidence is needed for the humanity of Jesus than the fact that He died?” (Etzel and Small, 89)

4 · One Person, Two Natures
ReadEtzel and Small, Everyday Theology

The hypostatic union is the doctrine that Jesus Christ is one person with two complete natures, fully God and fully man, united without confusion or change and without division or separation (Etzel and Small, 89). The person is the eternal Son; everything Christ does, the Son does.

The Chalcedonian Creed

The church stated the doctrine at the Council of Chalcedon (AD 451):

“We, then, following the holy Fathers … teach men to confess one and the same Son, our Lord Jesus Christ, the same perfect in Godhead and also perfect in manhood; truly God and truly man … consubstantial with the Father according to the Godhead, and consubstantial with us according to the Manhood; in all things like unto us, without sin.” (Etzel and Small, 89)

Jesus shares the same nature as God the Father and the same nature as humanity, equally and fully both.

(Etzel and Small, 89–90)

The Kenosis

Paul writes that Christ “emptied Himself” (Phil. 2:7). The kenosis (Greek kenoō, “to empty”) asks what that emptying involved. The answer is not that Jesus stopped being God: deity is His very nature, which He cannot set aside (Etzel and Gutierrez, 99). He emptied Himself by addition, not subtraction. Verse 7 explains itself: He emptied Himself “by taking the form of a servant, being born in the likeness of men.” He gave up no divine attribute, and He went on upholding the universe (Heb. 1:3) and forgiving sins (Mark 2:5). He humbled Himself in three ways (Etzel and Small, 90–92):

  1. He veiled His glory. He prayed for “the glory that I had with you before the world existed” (John 17:5). Glimpses of it appear at the Transfiguration (Matt. 17:2) and in Revelation (Rev. 1:12–16).
  2. He accepted human limitations. He grew tired, hungry, and thirsty, and in His human nature He lived in one place at a time.
  3. He relied on the Father and the Spirit. He “did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped” (Phil. 2:6); He did the Father’s will in the power of the Spirit.
“… He fulfilled His calling, obeyed the Father, resisted temptation, and performed His confirmatory miracles, fundamentally as a man empowered by the Spirit. He lived His life as one of us.” (Bruce Ware, The Man Christ Jesus, quoted in Etzel and Small, 91)

Why the Two Natures Matter

  • Being fully human, Jesus could die as one of us.
  • Being fully God, He was the perfect, spotless sacrifice for sin.
  • Being both, He is a high priest who can “sympathize with our weaknesses” (Heb. 4:15; Etzel and Small, 92).

Atonement carries the idea of a covering: the death of Jesus covers the sins of His people, satisfying the demands of a holy God once and for all (Etzel and Small, 97). Why did Jesus have to die? In Gethsemane He asked whether the cup could pass from Him (Luke 22:39–46). The answer begins not with our sin but with God’s nature.

Starting Point: The Nature of God

  • God is holy. Only the holy can stand in His presence (Lev. 11:44; 1 Pet. 1:16). His law is not arbitrary; it is an extension of His own nature. Sin is therefore rebellion against God Himself, and “the wages of sin is death” (Rom. 6:23).
  • God is love. “God shows His love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us” (Rom. 5:8).

God’s holiness demands judgment on sin; God’s love makes a way. At the cross both are satisfied: “The love of God and the holiness of God are not in conflict with one another” (Etzel and Small, 98).

Theories of the Atonement

TheoryCore claimOrigin
Ransom to SatanChrist’s death paid Satan, who held humanity captive.Origen (3rd c.)
Moral InfluenceThe cross is only an example of God’s love, not a payment.Peter Abelard (12th c.)
Penal SubstitutionChrist bore the penalty for sin in our place, satisfying God’s own justice.Historic evangelical position

(Etzel and Small, 96–97)

Etzel and Small affirm penal substitution: the answer to “Who required the payment?” is God. He required the death of Jesus to satisfy His own standard of holiness, and out of love He provided the substitute Himself (Etzel and Small, 96–97). The cross is also a victory, for Christ “disarmed the rulers and authorities” (Col. 2:15), and an example of love (1 Pet. 2:21); but the example has force only because the substitution is real.

Four Words That Define the Atonement

  1. Sacrifice: Jesus’ death was the once-for-all sacrifice the Old Testament system foreshadowed (Heb. 9:26; 10:10).
  2. Propitiation: the satisfying of wrath. “He is the propitiation for our sins” (1 John 2:2).
  3. Substitution: Jesus died in our place. “For our sake He made Him to be sin who knew no sin” (2 Cor. 5:21).
  4. Reconciliation: God removes the enmity between Himself and sinners (2 Cor. 5:18–19).

Together: the death of Christ (sacrifice) satisfied God’s wrath against sin (propitiation) by placing our guilt on Jesus (substitution), and so removed the enmity between God and sinners (reconciliation) (Etzel and Small, 97–98).

Redemption: The Marketplace Picture

Redemption means “to buy back” or “to purchase.” The New Testament uses three Greek words drawn from the marketplace and the slave market (Etzel and Gutierrez, 93):

Greek wordSenseReference
agorazō“to buy”: Christ paid the price for us1 Cor. 6:20
exagorazō“to buy out, rescue”: He removed us from bondageGal. 3:13
lytroō“to release, set free”: He freed us from sinTitus 2:14

The price was paid to God’s justice, not to Satan. Redemption summarizes the whole storyline of Scripture, often described as a four-part metanarrative: Creation, Fall, Redemption, Consummation (Etzel and Gutierrez, 94).

The resurrection (Greek anastasis, “a rising up”) is the bodily raising of Jesus from the dead, “the central, defining doctrine and claim of the Christian faith” (Etzel and Gutierrez, 109). “If Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile and you are still in your sins” (1 Cor. 15:17).

Its Importance in Scripture

  • Jesus predicted it more than once: the Son of Man must “be killed, and after three days rise again” (Mark 8:31; John 2:19–21).
  • It is the center of the gospel. Paul defines the gospel as Christ’s death, burial, and resurrection (1 Cor. 15:1–4).
  • It guarantees the believer’s hope. Jesus’ bodily resurrection secures the future bodily resurrection of all who trust Him, in imperishable, immortal bodies (1 Cor. 15:42–57; Etzel and Small, 100–101).
  • It declared Him the Son of God in power (Rom. 1:4) and showed that the Father accepted His sacrifice: He “was raised for our justification” (Rom. 4:25).

The Resurrection Was Bodily

The resurrection was physical, not merely spiritual (Etzel and Gutierrez, 110). After rising, Jesus ate before His disciples (Luke 24:42–43) and invited them to touch His hands and side (John 20:27). He rose in the same body that died, now glorified and no longer subject to death. A bodily resurrection is the pattern for the believer’s own.

False Views

TheoryClaimProblem
Lost or Stolen BodyThe disciples stole the body (Matt. 28:12–13).It does not explain why they would die for a lie they knew.
SwoonJesus only fainted, then revived.Roman executioners were experts; the spear wound (John 19:34) was fatal.
HallucinationThe disciples imagined the appearances.The appearances involved physical acts: eating, being touched.

(Etzel and Small, 102)

The Minimal Facts Approach

Gary Habermas and Michael Licona argue for the resurrection using only data granted by nearly all scholars, skeptical and Christian alike (Etzel and Small, 103). Skeptics do not believe in the resurrection; they grant the facts beneath it:

  1. Jesus died by crucifixion.
  2. His disciples believed they saw the risen Jesus. Peter, who had denied Him, was transformed.
  3. Paul, a persecutor of the church, was suddenly changed (Acts 8:1).
  4. James, Jesus’ skeptical brother, was suddenly changed (Mark 3:21; 1 Cor. 15:7), and later led the church in Jerusalem (Acts 15:13).

A fifth fact, the empty tomb, is granted by about 75 percent of scholars (Etzel and Small, 104). Together these facts make the bodily resurrection the most reasonable conclusion.

7 · Humiliation and Exaltation

The work of Christ is far larger than His three-year public ministry. It spans eternity, and theologians often map it as one arc: humiliation descending to the cross and exaltation rising to eternal glory (Etzel and Small, 105–106).

The Descent: Humiliation

StageDescription
Preincarnate gloryBefore creation the Son existed in glory and in fellowship within the Trinity (John 1:1–2; 17:5).
Incarnation“The Word became flesh” (John 1:14): the Son added a human nature, giving up nothing of His deity.
Earthly lifeHe lived a fully human life with its limits: growing, hungering, tiring (Luke 2:52).
CrucifixionThe lowest point: His atoning death, satisfying God’s wrath (Luke 23:44–45).

The Ascent: Exaltation

StageDescription
ResurrectionThe turning point: death defeated, the believer’s resurrection guaranteed (1 Cor. 15:20–28).
AscensionJesus returned to the Father (Acts 1:9), His earthly work complete, opening the way for the Spirit’s ministry and His preparation of a place for His people (John 14:2–3).
SessionHe sits at the Father’s right hand, sharing the Father’s throne, with all authority given to Him. He intercedes as our great high priest and waits until His enemies are made His footstool (Ps. 110:1; Heb. 4:14–16; 10:12–13). His reign on David’s throne is still future.
Second comingHe will return bodily for His people (Acts 1:11; 1 Cor. 15:52).
Future reignHe will rule visibly over His creation from the throne of David (Luke 1:32–33; Matt. 25:31). He is sovereign even now (Col. 1:17; Etzel and Small, 110).
Eternal gloryThe culmination: a new heaven and a new earth where God dwells with His people (Rev. 21:3–4).

The arc begins and ends in glory, with the cross at its lowest point. Paul draws the whole shape in Philippians 2:5–11 and turns it into a call: “Have this mind among yourselves, which is yours in Christ Jesus” (Phil. 2:5).

Why This Matters

Person and Work Cannot Be Separated

What Jesus did depends on who Jesus is. Only one who is fully God could offer an infinite, spotless sacrifice; only one who is fully human could die in our place. Deny either nature and the atonement collapses.

The Cross Holds Holiness and Love Together

The death of Jesus is not the story of an angry God appeased by a reluctant victim. It is the one God, holy and loving, satisfying His own justice by His own sacrifice. The cross is the meeting place of attributes that a lesser theology would set against each other.

The Resurrection Is the Hinge of History

Christianity rises or falls on an empty tomb. The resurrection authenticates everything Jesus claimed, secures the forgiveness of sin, and guarantees the believer’s own bodily resurrection. It is not a doctrine to affirm and shelve; it is “the Christian’s power for living” (Etzel and Gutierrez, 110).

Jesus Is the Point

Module 2 asked who God is. Module 3 gives the fullest answer: look at Jesus. He is the image of the invisible God, the Lamb who was slain, and the risen Lord who holds all authority. To know Him rightly is the center of the Christian faith, and the beginning of eternal life (John 17:3).

Check Yourself

Four questions for each deck. Choose an answer to check it. Nothing is saved.

Deck 1: Who Is Jesus?

  1. When Jesus asked, “Who do you say that I am?” how did Peter answer?

    Show answer

    Answer: “You are the Christ, the Son of the living God” The first three were what others were saying. Peter confessed that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of the living God (Matt. 16:16).

  2. Where did Jesus ask His disciples that question?

    Show answer

    Answer: In the district of Caesarea Philippi, a center of pagan worship. He asked it near Israel’s northern border, in a region known for the worship of Baal, Pan, and Caesar (Matt. 16:13).

  3. Christology studies Christ in two halves. What are they?

    Show answer

    Answer: His person and His work. Who He is (His person) and what He has done (His work). His deity and humanity belong to the first half.

  4. Why is Christology the center of the Christian faith, not one doctrine among many?

    Show answer

    Answer: What we believe about Jesus shapes what we believe about salvation, purpose, and eternity. “The point is that Jesus is the point.” What we believe about Him reaches into everything else.

Deck 2: Fully God

  1. When Jesus told the paralyzed man, “Son, your sins are forgiven,” why did the scribes object?

    Show answer

    Answer: Only God can forgive sins. “Who can forgive sins but God alone?” (Mark 2:7). They were right about that, and it is exactly the point: forgiving sins was one of the ways Jesus’ actions confirmed His deity.

  2. In Colossians 1, which works does Paul credit to Jesus?

    Show answer

    Answer: Creating all things and holding them together. “By Him all things were created… and in Him all things hold together” (Col. 1:16–17). Those are works of God alone.

  3. At Jesus’ trial before Pilate, what did the Jewish leaders say made Him worthy of death?

    Show answer

    Answer: He had made Himself the Son of God. “He ought to die because He has made Himself the Son of God” (John 19:7). His enemies understood His claim exactly.

  4. Why is “a good teacher, but not God” not a real option?

    Show answer

    Answer: A mere man who claimed what Jesus claimed would not be a great moral teacher. C. S. Lewis’s point: given what Jesus claimed, He is either the Son of God or something far worse. He did not leave the middle option open.

Deck 3: Fully Human

  1. When the Son became man, what happened to His deity?

    Show answer

    Answer: He gave up nothing of His deity and added a complete human nature. The incarnation is an addition, not an exchange. The Son remains fully God and is now also fully man, forever.

  2. What does the doctrine of the virgin birth teach?

    Show answer

    Answer: The Holy Spirit conceived Jesus in Mary’s womb without a human father. It is about Jesus’ conception, not Mary’s. It fulfills Isaiah 7:14: “Behold, the virgin shall conceive and bear a son.”

  3. Which of these is not one of the three things the virgin birth helps establish?

    Show answer

    Answer: Mary’s lifelong virginity. Etzel and Gutierrez name the first three. The angel tied the child’s holiness to the Spirit’s work: “the child to be born will be called holy” (Luke 1:35).

  4. Which fact gives the plainest proof that Jesus was truly human?

    Show answer

    Answer: He died. “What more evidence is needed for the humanity of Jesus than the fact that He died?” (Etzel and Small, p. 89; Luke 23:46).

Deck 4: One Person, Two Natures

  1. How does the hypostatic union describe Jesus Christ?

    Show answer

    Answer: One person with two complete natures. He is one person, the eternal Son, who is fully God and fully man. The two natures are not mixed together, and the person is not divided.

  2. Someone says Jesus only seemed to have a real body. Which error is that?

    Show answer

    Answer: Docetism. Docetism denies His humanity: Jesus only appeared to be human. Nestorianism makes Him two persons, Adoptionism says He became God’s Son later, and Arianism denies that He is fully God.

  3. Philippians 2:7 says Christ “emptied Himself.” What does that mean?

    Show answer

    Answer: He took the form of a servant, adding a human nature without giving up any divine attribute. He emptied Himself by addition, not by subtraction. The first choice has a name of its own: Kenoticism.

  4. Why must Jesus be both fully God and fully man to save us?

    Show answer

    Answer: As man He could die in our place, and as God He was the perfect, spotless sacrifice. Deny either nature and the atonement falls apart. Being both, He is also a high priest who sympathizes with our weaknesses (Heb. 4:15).

Deck 5: The Cross

  1. Where does the deeper answer to “Why did Jesus have to die?” begin?

    Show answer

    Answer: With the nature of God, who is both holy and loving. “Because of our sin” is true but incomplete. God’s holiness demands judgment on sin, and His love makes a way.

  2. In penal substitution, who required the payment for sin?

    Show answer

    Answer: God, whose justice requires it, and who in love provided the substitute. God required the death of Jesus to satisfy His own justice, and He gave the substitute Himself (Etzel and Small, pp. 96–97).

  3. What does reconciliation mean in the atonement?

    Show answer

    Answer: God removes the enmity between Himself and sinners. “In Christ God was reconciling the world to Himself” (2 Cor. 5:19).

  4. Redemption sums up the whole storyline of Scripture. In what order does that story run?

    Show answer

    Answer: Creation → Fall → Redemption → Consummation. Creation, Fall, Redemption, Consummation is the four-part metanarrative (Etzel and Gutierrez, p. 94).

Deck 6: The Resurrection

  1. How does Paul sum up the gospel in 1 Corinthians 15?

    Show answer

    Answer: Christ’s death, burial, and resurrection. “That Christ died for our sins… that He was buried, that He was raised on the third day” (1 Cor. 15:3–4).

  2. Romans 4:25 says Jesus “was raised for our justification.” What did the resurrection show?

    Show answer

    Answer: That the Father accepted His sacrifice. The resurrection is the Father’s verdict on the cross. It also declared Jesus to be the Son of God in power (Rom. 1:4).

  3. How do we know the resurrection was bodily and not merely spiritual?

    Show answer

    Answer: He ate before His disciples and invited them to touch Him. “A spirit does not have flesh and bones as you see that I have” (Luke 24:39). He took fish and ate it before them (vv. 42–43).

  4. Paul calls the risen Christ “the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep.” What does that promise?

    Show answer

    Answer: That His people will be raised as He was. Firstfruits are the first part of the harvest, the pledge of the rest. His resurrection guarantees ours (1 Cor. 15:20).

Deck 7: Humiliation and Exaltation

  1. Where does the arc of Christ’s work turn upward?

    Show answer

    Answer: At the resurrection. The cross is the lowest point. The resurrection is the turning point, where death is defeated and the ascent begins.

  2. Seated at the Father’s right hand, what is Christ doing now?

    Show answer

    Answer: Interceding for His people as their high priest. “He always lives to make intercession for them” (Heb. 7:25). He holds all authority now, and His reign on David’s throne is still to come.

  3. What did the ascension mark?

    Show answer

    Answer: The completion of His earthly work and the start of the Spirit’s new ministry. His work on earth was finished. He went to the Father, the Spirit’s ministry opened, and He went to prepare a place for His people (Acts 1:9; John 14:2–3).

  4. Paul traces the whole arc in Philippians 2. What does he turn it into?

    Show answer

    Answer: A call to share Christ’s mind and attitude. “Have this mind among yourselves, which is yours in Christ Jesus” (Phil. 2:5).
