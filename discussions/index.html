Discussion Guide

How discussions work, how to write your thread and your reply, and the mistakes that cost points.

How Discussions Work

Every discussion in this course works the same way. Each one is worth 50 points.

  • Your thread · Thursday. Answer the prompt in 200–300 words by 11:59 PM (ET) on Thursday of the module week.
  • Your reply · Monday. Reply to one classmate’s thread in at least 200 words by 11:59 PM (ET) on Monday.
  • Post first. You will see your classmates’ threads only after you post your own.
  • How it’s graded. Content counts 35 points: answering the prompt, support from course materials or Scripture, and a reply that moves the conversation forward. Writing style and mechanics count 15: clear flow and clean spelling, grammar, and punctuation.
  • Formatting. Use the style of your degree program even in discussions: APA, MLA, or Turabian. If you cite a source, end your post with the matching list: References, Works Cited, or Bibliography.

Writing Your Thread

A strong thread is personal, specific, and grounded.

  • Answer every part. Each prompt asks two or three questions. Address each one; a skipped part costs content points.
  • Make it yours. Prompts ask what you think and what you have experienced. A general statement anyone could write, or a definition lifted from a textbook, says little about you. Be specific: a church, a class, a conversation, a question you still carry.
  • Ground it. Support your main point with the course materials or Scripture. Choose one passage and explain how it bears on your point, rather than listing several in parentheses.
  • Organize it. Write in paragraphs with a clear flow: your point, its support, and what it means for you.
  • Cite it. Cite in your degree program’s style. For Scripture, see “How do I cite Scripture?” in the essay guide.

Writing Your Reply

A reply carries the conversation forward.

  • Respond to what they wrote. Pick a specific point from your classmate’s thread and engage it.
  • Move it forward. Do at least one of these: ask a question that probes their thinking; connect their point to the course materials, Scripture, or your own experience; or carry their idea a step further.
  • Go beyond agreement. “I agree” or “I like how you said…” doesn’t advance the dialogue. Neither does summarizing their thread or commenting on their writing.
  • Ask, then explain. A follow-up question is a good move, but a question alone is not a reply. Say why the question matters.
  • Reach 200 words. Ground your reply, as you did your thread, in course materials or Scripture where it fits.

For models of threads and replies, see Prof. Maitland’s postings in each discussion.

Common Mistakes

In the thread

Key ideaSpecific and grounded.
  • Too general. It reads like a reflection anyone could have written. Name your own church, class, question, or plan.
  • A list without depth. Several points are named, but none is developed. Choose one or two and explain them.
  • An example left unexplained. Show how your example illustrates your point.
  • No course materials or Scripture. Ground your main point in a reading or one passage.
  • A part of the prompt skipped. Check each question before you post.

In the reply

Key ideaMove the conversation forward.
  • Praise or agreement alone. “Great post!” and “I agree” don’t advance the dialogue.
  • A summary of their thread. They know what they wrote; respond to it.
  • A critique of their writing. Engage their ideas, not their style.
  • Off the point. Respond to what your classmate actually wrote, not to the topic in general.
  • Under 200 words.

Everywhere

  • Posting late. Discussions follow the course’s late policy, which is up to 10% off within a week of the due date and up to 20% off within two weeks. A late thread is penalized even if your reply is on time. If you can’t post on time, email Prof. Maitland right away.
  • Over or under length. The thread runs 200–300 words, and the reply at least 200.
  • Formatting skipped. Your degree program’s style applies to discussions too.
  • Lowercase sacred terms. Capitalize Bible, Scripture, Old Testament, New Testament, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.
  • Casual language. Keep to an academic register, even in a discussion.
