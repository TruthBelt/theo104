1 · Who Are We?
ReadEtzel and Small, Everyday Theology
  • 21. Who Am I? pp. 149–154
Opens in Logos.

This module studies two doctrines that belong together. Biblical anthropology (from the Greek anthrōpos, “man”) is the study of human beings: our origin, identity, nature, and makeup. Hamartiology (from the Greek hamartia, “missing the mark”) is the study of the nature and effects of sin. When we look closely at ourselves, we see signs of a design fashioned by our Creator, yet we also see that things are not what they ought to be, in the world and in ourselves.

Identity Determines Destiny

“Identity determines destiny! A person cannot become something they do not believe they are or aspire to be” (Etzel and Small, 149). How we answer Who am I? shapes how we live, yet most people forge ahead without pausing to ask. Much of our identity is formed by things we did not choose: our bodies and DNA, our families, our cultures and subcultures. Becoming aware of these influences is the starting place (Etzel and Small, 149–50).

Competing Stories for the Soul

Advertisers, classrooms, pulpits, families, and friends press rival visions of the self upon us, and these visions “often offer us too little” (Etzel and Small, 151):

StoryThe ClaimWhat It Misses
We are what we produceIdentity is financial security and productivityReduces the person to economic output
We are our gender and sexualityIdentity is a bundle of sexual impulsesReduces the person to desire
We are the sum of our relationshipsIdentity is found in our connectionsLeaves many “connected” yet alone
We are on a quest for freedomIdentity is autonomy from all restraintMistakes self-rule for flourishing

Each story names a real longing, for purpose, intimacy, belonging, and freedom, but none can deliver what it promises (Etzel and Small, 151–52).

Made on Purpose

The Bible begins by telling us where we came from, who made us, and why we exist. Human creation was a divine act: “So God created man in His own image, in the image of God He created him; male and female He created them” (Gen. 1:27). Since we were made on purpose, our lives have meaning and purpose (Etzel and Small, 152). Three foundational truths follow (Etzel and Small, 153):

  • Humanity was created by God, not by “cosmic random selection,” but by a personal God who intended to make us.
  • Humanity was placed in a world designed by God. The garden was sacred space, not neutral space: a place God Himself visited, where every activity was to be done for His glory.
  • Humanity was given a role to serve God. Adam was put in the garden “to work it and keep it” (Gen. 2:15). From this comes the Christian idea of vocation, or calling. Whatever our work, whether fast-food worker, stay-at-home mother, nurse, mechanic, or lawyer, it is a platform to love our neighbor and serve God, not a distraction from our “real” identity.

More Than Nature

This origin stands against naturalism, the belief that nothing exists beyond the physical world. Naturalism cannot be proven: to prove it, one would have to examine all places at all moments and show that nothing more than nature exists. Yet it is “pressed upon the masses with a much greater dogmatic quality than most messages from churches” (Etzel and Small, 163).

2 · The Image of God
ReadEtzel and Small, Everyday Theology
  • 22. What Does It Mean to Be Created in God’s Image? pp. 155–162
Etzel and Gutierrez, Theology Applied
  • 11. Image of God pp. 61–63
Opens in Logos.

The image of God (Latin imago Dei) is the doctrine that human beings are made to resemble and represent God. Humans, and humans alone, bear it (Gen. 1:26; Etzel and Small, 155). It belongs to human nature itself: “It refers to something a human is rather than something a human has or does” (Erickson, quoted in Etzel and Gutierrez, 61). It is also, in Etzel and Gutierrez’s words, “one of the most misunderstood doctrines in the Bible” (Etzel and Gutierrez, 61).

What an Image Is

The Hebrew word for image, tselem, is often used of a physical representation, like the statue a conquering king set up in a foreign land so the people would know whom they served (Etzel and Gutierrez, 61). To the ancient mind, an image conveyed a god’s authority over a territory. God wanted no man-made images; He had made His own image-bearers to serve under His authority in His world (Etzel and Small, 156).

A Multileveled Concept

Scripture gives hints rather than one tidy definition, so the image is best understood as a multileveled concept (Etzel and Small, 156–57):

AspectMeaningScripture
Dominion and stewardshipWe reflect God by ruling and caring for His world, never exploiting it, since the world is HisGen. 1:28
RelationshipMade male and female, we mirror a God who exists in relationship as Father, Son, and Spirit; marriage is its highest expressionGen. 2:24
Relating to GodSomething in our makeup lets us know and love God: the substantive view, which points to the soulGen. 1:27

The Dignity of Every Person

Because every human bears God’s image, all share equally in dignity and worth. That worth rests not on what a person does, how they look, or how useful they are, but on being an image-bearer (Etzel and Small, 159). God “made from one man every nation of mankind” (Acts 17:26), so racism has no place in the Christian life (Etzel and Small, 160). Christians give special care to the vulnerable: the poor, the widow, the orphan, the aged, the refugee, and the unborn (Etzel and Small, 161–62).

Marred, Not Erased

The fall did not erase the image; it diminished it. After the fall, God still grounds the sanctity of human life in the image: “Whoever sheds the blood of man, by man shall his blood be shed, for God made man in His own image” (Gen. 9:6). James still speaks of “people who are made in the likeness of God” (Jas. 3:9). Yet Adam “fathered a son in his own likeness, after his image” (Gen. 5:3), so every descendant of Adam bears both God’s image and Adam’s. Part of us longs to glorify God; another part wants to go its own way (Etzel and Small, 157).

Christ and the Restoration of the Image

Christ is “the image of the invisible God” (Col. 1:15). In Him we see both who God is and who we are meant to become (Etzel and Small, 158). Salvation restores our humanity rather than diminishing it: Christianity “asks us not to die to our humanity, but to die to our old self so that our new self can emerge” (Etzel and Small, 158). The new self “is being renewed in knowledge after the image of its creator” (Col. 3:10), and believers are “predestined to be conformed to the image of His Son” (Rom. 8:29). The restoration is real now but finished only in glory: “When He appears we shall be like Him, because we shall see Him as He is” (1 John 3:2).

The arc of the image: given in creation → marred in the fall → displayed perfectly in Christ → being restored in the believer → fully restored in glory.
3 · Body and Soul
ReadEtzel and Small, Everyday Theology
  • 23. What Does It Mean to Possess a Soul? pp. 163–166
Etzel and Gutierrez, Theology Applied
  • 15. Material/Immaterial pp. 81–83
Opens in Logos.

The soul is the immaterial aspect of a human being. It enlivens the body (Gen. 2:7), carries our intellectual, relational, and emotional capacity, and can live on after the death of the body (2 Cor. 5:8; Etzel and Small, 163). Jesus assumed both a material and an immaterial aspect: “Do not fear those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul” (Matt. 10:28).

Three Reasons to Affirm the Soul

Thinkers across the centuries, not only theologians, have judged it probable that humans have an immaterial aspect (Etzel and Small, 164):

  1. Causation. You think a thought, and your hand rises. Something non-physical produces something physical.
  2. Freedom. Our choices are our own: we weigh reasons and act on what we desire. If we were only a closed set of biological connections, we would be hardwired—chemistry running its course, with no one there to choose.
  3. Unified experience. We see, hear, and feel many things at once, yet experience them as one whole, which points to an immaterial aspect that unifies them.

Three Views of Our Makeup

ViewClaimKey Texts
TrichotomyThree distinct substances: body, soul, and spirit1 Thess. 5:23; Heb. 4:12
DichotomyTwo parts: the material body and the immaterial soul or spiritMatt. 10:28; Gen. 2:7
MonismOne substance that cannot be divided into partsStresses human unity

Scripture uses several words for our makeup, often interchangeably. Jesus said, “Now is My soul troubled” (John 12:27), and on the cross, “Father, into Your hands I commit My spirit!” (Luke 23:46). The human spirit can be defiled (2 Cor. 7:1), and the Holy Spirit dwells with us without becoming part of us, so trichotomy confuses the two (Etzel and Small, 165). Scripture distinguishes the material from the immaterial, so monism overreaches; yet the normal state of a human being is both together. God formed the body from dust and breathed in the breath of life, and the man became a living creature (Gen. 2:7). The separation of body and soul at death “is an unnatural tragedy, which will be remedied when the body is resurrected” (ESV Study Bible, quoted in Etzel and Gutierrez, 81).

Two Errors to Avoid

  • Reductionism: collapsing the person into nothing but body and brain, the error of naturalism.
  • A low view of the body: treating the soul as good and the body as bad. The whole person, material and immaterial, was affected by the fall, and physical desires are not sinful in themselves (Etzel and Gutierrez, 81–82).

Because people are body and soul, the gospel addresses the whole person, and ministry meets physical needs as well as spiritual ones (Etzel and Gutierrez, 82). “You were bought with a price. So glorify God in your body” (1 Cor. 6:20).

Soul Work

Because the soul is real, prayer, biblical meditation, and the other spiritual disciplines are not merely subjective, feel-good activities. They nourish the deepest part of us, as food nourishes the body (Etzel and Small, 166).

4 · The Heart
ReadEtzel and Gutierrez, Theology Applied
  • 13. Heart pp. 71–73
  • 14. Human Personality pp. 77–79
Opens in Logos.

In Scripture the heart (Hebrew leb, Greek kardia) is not the organ that pumps blood but the inner person, the core from which thinking, feeling, and choosing flow. It “stands for the inner being of man, the man himself. As such, it is the fountain of all he does” (Vine’s, quoted in Etzel and Gutierrez, 71). “From it flow the springs of life” (Prov. 4:23), and “out of the heart come evil thoughts” (Matt. 15:19).

When Jesus commands, “You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart” (Matt. 22:37), He means with all that you are (Etzel and Gutierrez, 71). That is no small thing, because the heart is also where our deepest problem lies: “The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately sick” (Jer. 17:9). God promised Israel a new heart: “I will remove the heart of stone from your flesh and give you a heart of flesh” (Ezek. 36:26). David prayed, “Create in me a clean heart, O God” (Ps. 51:10), and in Christ the believer is “a new creation” (2 Cor. 5:17; Etzel and Gutierrez, 72).

Intellect, Emotion, and Will

The human personality works through three aspects (Etzel and Gutierrez, 77):

AspectWhat It DoesIn Scripture
IntellectThinks and reasonsThe Bereans were “examining the Scriptures daily” (Acts 17:11)
EmotionFeels and respondsDavid’s despair in Psalm 51; Jesus weeping at Lazarus’s tomb (John 11:35)
WillChooses and acts“Choose this day whom you will serve” (Josh. 24:15)

Of the three, the will is the most vital, because it is the point of decision: we can know the right thing and feel that it is right, but until we decide to act, nothing happens (Etzel and Gutierrez, 78). The believer grows by tying his thinking to God’s truth, so that his emotions follow it and his will chooses wisely: “Be transformed by the renewal of your mind” (Rom. 12:2). This is how a heart God has made new grows. No amount of training gives anyone a new heart.

5 · The Fall and Original Sin
ReadEtzel and Small, Everyday Theology
  • 24. What Is Wrong with Me? pp. 167–170
Etzel and Gutierrez, Theology Applied
  • 12. Sin pp. 65–68
Opens in Logos.

We like problems we can solve. But if the problem lies in the deepest part of us, in a condition we did not choose and cannot cure, then we must cry out to God for His mercy. Sin “is a problem that we cannot solve” (Etzel and Small, 167).

The Fall

Sin entered the world through a real event involving real people (Gen. 3:1–7). God gave Adam and Eve all they needed, and one command (Etzel and Small, 168). The serpent was a real animal, but it did not speak in its own power (Etzel and Small, 169); later Scripture names the speaker as Satan, “that ancient serpent, who is called the devil” (Rev. 12:9). He questioned God’s word (“Did God actually say…?”), then denied it (“You will not surely die”), suggesting that God was restricting life rather than protecting it. Satan tempted; he did not cause the sin. Eve was deceived, but “Adam was not deceived” (1 Tim. 2:14). Both disobeyed, and the guilt was theirs. Humanity became a shadow of its former self, its God-given dignity twisted and distorted by sin (Etzel and Small, 169).

What Sin Is

“Sin is basically anything that removes God from His proper place in our lives”: violating His commands, or breaking relationship with Him to go our own way (Etzel and Small, 171). The Bible gives no single definition but many words. The New Testament uses six Greek terms; the most common, hamartia, means “to miss the mark,” like an archer who misses the bull’s-eye (Etzel and Gutierrez, 65). Scripture pictures sin as missing the mark (Rom. 3:23), crossing the line (1 John 3:4), twisting God’s design, and rebelling against Him (Isa. 1:2). Closely connected is idolatry: anything in which we place our hope, confidence, and worship, removing God from His proper place (Etzel and Small, 171). Sin can be a matter of action or intention, of ignorance or open defiance (Etzel and Small, 172).

Original Sin

Original sin is the condition of sinfulness that precedes and produces actual sin; it explains how sin originates in each one of us (Etzel and Small, 169). Adam’s sin was not his alone: “Sin came into the world through one man, and death through sin, and so death spread to all men because all sinned” (Rom. 5:12). David confessed, “In sin did my mother conceive me” (Ps. 51:5). Views of Adam’s effect range from Adam as a bad example to Adam’s sin resulting in our guilt, and the New Testament points to the second. As humanity’s representative, Adam “casts the ballot of sinfulness for all humanity” (Etzel and Small, 169–70): “by the one man’s disobedience the many were made sinners” (Rom. 5:19). We are sinners by position (Adam represents us), by nature (a corrupt condition from birth), and by action (the sins we commit).

The Sun and Its Rays

Draw a child’s sun: a circle with rays shooting out. The circle is the sinful nature, equally present in every person. The rays are sinful acts, and people draw different numbers of them. Some commit more visible sins and some fewer, and society punishes them differently, but in God’s eyes every person has the same sinful nature (Etzel and Gutierrez, 66–67).

6 · How Deep Sin Goes
ReadEtzel and Small, Everyday Theology
  • 25. What Is Sin? How Can I Defeat It? pp. 171–176
Opens in Logos.

“Christianity does not say that we are as sinful as we could be” (Etzel and Small, 175). A real, God-given morality remains in every person: “the work of the law is written on their hearts, while their conscience also bears witness” (Rom. 2:15). But every part of us has been touched by sin. This is total depravity: not maximal wickedness, but corruption that reaches every part of us. Before the flood, “every intention of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually” (Gen. 6:5).

Paul’s indictment leaves no one out:

“None is righteous, no, not one; no one understands; no one seeks for God. All have turned aside; together they have become worthless; no one does good, not even one” (Rom. 3:10–12).

The law speaks “so that every mouth may be stopped, and the whole world may be held accountable to God” (Rom. 3:19). By God’s standard, everyone has missed the mark (Etzel and Small, 175).

Total Inability

“The person apart from God is spiritually dead and has total inability apart from God’s intervention” (Etzel and Small, 175). We were “dead in the trespasses and sins” (Eph. 2:1); the mind set on the flesh “does not submit to God’s law; indeed, it cannot” (Rom. 8:7). We can change our actions, the rays of the sun, but “you are unable to clean up your sinful nature that is resident in your heart.… Only God can touch that part of your soul” (Etzel and Gutierrez, 67). The teaching is meant “to not depress us but to lead us to God and our need for Him” (Etzel and Small, 175–76).

7 · Temptation and Hope
ReadEtzel and Small, Everyday Theology
  • 25. What Is Sin? How Can I Defeat It? pp. 172–176
Opens in Logos.

God Tests, but Tempts No One

Temptation is the lure toward wrong thoughts and actions. God tests everyone but tempts no one: “God cannot be tempted with evil, and He Himself tempts no one. But each person is tempted when he is lured and enticed by his own desire” (Jas. 1:13–14). Temptation comes from our own sinful heart, the world, and sometimes Satan himself; it is “the wrong way out of a test” (Etzel and Small, 173). With the temptation God “will also provide the way of escape” (1 Cor. 10:13).

Jesus Shows the Way Out

Jesus “in every respect has been tempted as we are, yet without sin” (Heb. 4:15). He could not sin, yet His temptations were real, and He met them as a man, “led up by the Spirit” (Matt. 4:1) and answering each from Scripture (Matt. 4:1–11; Etzel and Small, 173–74):

TemptationSatan’s OfferJesus’s Answer
ProvisionTurn stones into bread: meet your own need now“Man shall not live by bread alone” (Matt. 4:4)
VindicationThrow Yourself from the temple: make God prove who You are“You shall not put the Lord your God to the test” (Matt. 4:7)
The kingdomAll the kingdoms of the world, in exchange for worship“You shall worship the Lord your God and Him only shall you serve” (Matt. 4:10)

Following Jesus means trusting God to fulfill our desires, waiting on His timing for our validation, and rejecting every idolatry that offers a shortcut to power (Etzel and Small, 174).

Sin Grows

“Desire when it has conceived gives birth to sin, and sin when it is fully grown brings forth death” (Jas. 1:15). The first act of disobedience is the hardest; each repetition dulls the conscience (1 Tim. 4:2) and emboldens the next, until sin’s control becomes obvious (Etzel and Small, 174–75).

The Hope

The honest diagnosis of sin is not the last word. “All that sin has taken God can restore. This is the hope of the Christian message” (Etzel and Small, 176). In the mirror we see a person of dignity, marred by sin, in need of God, which is exactly where the doctrine of salvation begins in Module 6.

Why This Matters

We Are Made in God’s Image

Human worth is not earned by performance, appearance, or usefulness; it is grounded in the fact that every person bears God’s image. This is the foundation for human dignity, the equality of all people, and the Christian’s care for the vulnerable. To diminish the image in anyone is to dishonor the God whose image they bear.

We Are Body and Soul

We are neither merely bodies and brains nor souls trapped in worthless flesh. God made the whole person and redeems the whole person, body and soul, through the resurrection. This calls for godliness in every part of life and a ministry that meets both physical and spiritual needs.

Sin Gives an Honest Diagnosis

The doctrine of sin tells the truth about what is wrong with the world and with us. The problem is not merely economic, social, or technological; it is rooted in the human heart, in a condition we did not choose and cannot cure. Total depravity and total inability strip away every illusion of self-sufficiency.

Both Doctrines Point to Christ

Humanity and sin are two sides of one coin, and both find their answer in Jesus. He is the perfect image we were made to reflect and are being restored toward; He is the sinless one who faced our temptations and bore our guilt. To study what we are and what has gone wrong is, finally, to be driven to Him.

Check Yourself

Four questions for each deck. Choose an answer to check it. Nothing is saved.

Deck 1: Who Are We?

  1. What does biblical anthropology study?

    Show answer

    Answer: Human beings: our origin, identity, nature, and makeup. From the Greek anthrōpos, “man.” Its companion, hamartiology, studies sin; the two are sides of one coin.

  2. One competing story says we are what we produce. What does it miss?

    Show answer

    Answer: It reduces the person to economic output. Each competing story names a real longing but cannot fulfill it. They “often offer us too little” (Etzel and Small, p. 151).

  3. What made the garden sacred space rather than neutral space?

    Show answer

    Answer: God Himself visited it, and every activity there was to be done for His glory. God placed humanity in a world He designed, a space He visited, where all of life was to be lived for His glory (Etzel and Small, p. 153).

  4. Why can naturalism not be proven?

    Show answer

    Answer: To prove it, one would have to examine all places at all moments. No one can show that nothing exists beyond nature. Yet naturalism “is pressed upon the masses with a much greater dogmatic quality than most messages from churches” (Etzel and Small, p. 163).

Deck 2: The Image of God

  1. What did the Hebrew word for image, tselem, often describe?

    Show answer

    Answer: A physical representation, like the statue a king set up in a conquered land. Humanity was made to represent God on earth, to point one another to the King of Kings (Etzel and Gutierrez, p. 61).

  2. On what does a person’s worth rest?

    Show answer

    Answer: Being made in God’s image. Every person bears God’s image, so all share equally in dignity and worth. God “made from one man every nation of mankind” (Acts 17:26).

  3. After the fall, what happened to the image of God in humanity?

    Show answer

    Answer: It was marred but not erased. After the fall, God still grounds the value of human life in His image (Gen. 9:6), and James speaks of “people who are made in the likeness of God” (Jas. 3:9).

  4. Where is the image of God seen most clearly?

    Show answer

    Answer: In Jesus Christ. “He is the image of the invisible God” (Col. 1:15). In Jesus we see both who God is and who we are meant to become.

Deck 3: Body and Soul

  1. Which view holds that a human being has two parts, a material body and an immaterial soul or spirit?

    Show answer

    Answer: Dichotomy. Dichotomy treats soul and spirit as essentially the same. Trichotomy counts three substances, and monism counts one (Etzel and Gutierrez, p. 81).

  2. Why do the textbooks doubt that soul and spirit are separate parts?

    Show answer

    Answer: Scripture often uses the two words interchangeably. Jesus said, “Now is My soul troubled” (John 12:27), and on the cross, “Father, into Your hands I commit My spirit!” (Luke 23:46). Both words name the same inner person.

  3. Why is the separation of body and soul at death called “an unnatural tragedy”?

    Show answer

    Answer: Because the normal state of a human being is body and soul together. God made the whole person, body and soul, as one living being (Gen. 2:7). The separation “will be remedied when the body is resurrected” (ESV Study Bible, quoted in Etzel and Gutierrez, p. 81).

  4. Comparing prayer and meditation on Scripture with eating makes what point?

    Show answer

    Answer: They nourish the soul as food nourishes the body. Few would say, “If eating your food helps you, then you should do it.” Spiritual disciplines are not merely subjective; they feed the deepest part of us (Etzel and Small, p. 166).

Deck 4: The Heart

  1. In Scripture, what does the word “heart” name?

    Show answer

    Answer: The inner person, the core from which thinking, feeling, and choosing flow. “Keep your heart with all vigilance, for from it flow the springs of life” (Prov. 4:23). Our thoughts, desires, words, and actions all come from it.

  2. What does it mean to love God “with all your heart”?

    Show answer

    Answer: With all that you are. Jesus’ command in Matt. 22:37 calls for the whole person: to love Him with all your heart is to love Him with all that you are (Etzel and Gutierrez, p. 71).

  3. Where did Jesus place the source of sin?

    Show answer

    Answer: In the heart. “Out of the heart come evil thoughts” (Matt. 15:19). Even a lustful look is adultery “in his heart” (Matt. 5:28).

  4. Who gives a person a new heart?

    Show answer

    Answer: God. “I will give you a new heart” (Ezek. 36:26). Renewing the mind (Rom. 12:2) is how a heart God has made new grows; no amount of training gives anyone a new heart.

Deck 5: The Fall and Original Sin

  1. What does hamartia, the New Testament’s most common word for sin, mean?

    Show answer

    Answer: To miss the mark. Picture an archer whose arrow misses the bull’s-eye. “All have sinned and fall short of the glory of God” (Rom. 3:23; Etzel and Gutierrez, p. 65).

  2. Who spoke through the serpent in the garden?

    Show answer

    Answer: Satan, “that ancient serpent” The serpent was a real animal that did not speak in its own power (Etzel and Small, p. 169). Later Scripture names the speaker: “that ancient serpent, who is called the devil and Satan” (Rev. 12:9).

  3. In the sun-and-rays illustration, what does the circle stand for?

    Show answer

    Answer: The sinful nature, the same in every person. The rays are sinful acts, and they vary from person to person. The circle is the sinful nature, which God sees in everyone (Etzel and Gutierrez, p. 67).

  4. Adam acted as humanity’s representative. What follows for us?

    Show answer

    Answer: We are sinners by position, by nature, and by action. Adam “casts the ballot of sinfulness for all humanity” (Etzel and Small, p. 170). “By the one man’s disobedience the many were made sinners” (Rom. 5:19).

Deck 6: How Deep Sin Goes

  1. What does total depravity teach?

    Show answer

    Answer: Every part of a person has been affected by sin. “Christianity does not say that we are as sinful as we could be” (Etzel and Small, p. 175). It says sin’s corruption reaches every part of us.

  2. Why do people without God still show real morality?

    Show answer

    Answer: The work of God’s law is written on their hearts, and their conscience bears witness. A real, God-given morality remains in every person (Rom. 2:15), even though sin has touched every part of us.

  3. What is total inability?

    Show answer

    Answer: Being spiritually dead and unable to save oneself apart from God’s intervention. “The person apart from God is spiritually dead and has total inability apart from God’s intervention” (Etzel and Small, p. 175). We were “dead in the trespasses and sins” (Eph. 2:1).

  4. What is the teaching on human sinfulness meant to do?

    Show answer

    Answer: Lead us to God and our need for Him. The teaching is “to not depress us but to lead us to God and our need for Him” (Etzel and Small, pp. 175–76).

Deck 7: Temptation and Hope

  1. How do Etzel and Small describe temptation?

    Show answer

    Answer: As the wrong way out of a test. “Temptation is merely the wrong way out of a test” (Etzel and Small, p. 173). With the temptation, God “will also provide the way of escape” (1 Cor. 10:13).

  2. Jesus “in every respect has been tempted as we are, yet without sin.” What else is true of His temptations?

    Show answer

    Answer: He could not sin, yet His temptations were real. He met temptation as a man, “led up by the Spirit” (Matt. 4:1), answering each one with “It is written” (Heb. 4:15).

  3. When Satan offered Jesus all the kingdoms of the world, what did Jesus refuse?

    Show answer

    Answer: To gain a kingdom through idolatry. “You shall worship the Lord your God and Him only shall you serve” (Matt. 4:10). Following Jesus means rejecting every idolatry that offers a shortcut to power (Etzel and Small, p. 174).

  4. According to James, where does sin lead when it is left to grow?

    Show answer

    Answer: It brings forth death. “Desire when it has conceived gives birth to sin, and sin when it is fully grown brings forth death” (Jas. 1:15). Each repetition dulls the conscience.
