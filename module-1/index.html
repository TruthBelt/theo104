1 · What Is Theology?
ReadMillard Erickson, Introducing Christian Doctrine (supplemental)
  • 1. What Is Theology?
Opens in Logos.

Prolegomena (Greek pro, “before,” and legō, “to say”) covers what must be settled before the study begins: why study theology at all, what assumptions we bring, and what definitions and methods guide the work.

What Is Theology?

The word joins theos (“God”) and logos (“words” or “rational discourse”). At its simplest, theology is the study of God and His self-revelation (Etzel and Small, 8). Erickson defines it as “the careful, systematic study, analysis, and statement of Christian doctrine,” and doctrine as the most fundamental beliefs a Christian holds about God, His actions, humanity, and what He has done to bring us to Himself. Far from dry, doctrine answers the questions everyone asks: Who am I? What is the ultimate meaning of the universe? Where am I going?

Five Characteristics

  • Biblical. Its primary content comes from the Old and New Testaments.
  • Systematic. It draws together what the whole Bible says on a topic into one coherent whole.
  • Cultural. It relates Scripture’s teaching to what other disciplines say about the same subject.
  • Contemporary. It restates timeless truth in a form people today can understand.
  • Practical. Like Paul, it shows how doctrine applies to life (1 Thess. 4:16–18) (Erickson).

Types and Branches

Etzel and Small name four types of theology: biblical (the contributions of individual authors, genres, and themes), philosophical (God studied through reason and the evidence of the created world), historical (how doctrine developed through church history), and systematic (God’s Word and God’s world organized by topic) (Etzel and Small, 6). This course follows a systematic approach, grounded in biblical evidence.

Systematic theology divides into branches: Bibliology (the Bible), Theology Proper (God), Christology (Jesus Christ), Pneumatology (the Holy Spirit), Anthropology (humanity), Hamartiology (sin), Soteriology (salvation), Ecclesiology (the church), and Eschatology (last things). The branches connect. Your view of the Bible shapes every other branch; your view of Jesus shapes your view of salvation; your view of sin shows why salvation is needed (Etzel and Small, 7).

ReadEtzel and Gutierrez, Theology Applied
  • Introduction: Theology Must Work in Real Life pp. ix–x
Opens in Logos.

Why Doctrine Is Not Optional

  • Correct belief is essential to a relationship with God. Whoever comes to God must believe that He exists and rewards those who seek Him (Heb. 11:6), and salvation is tied to believing that God raised Jesus from the dead (Rom. 10:9–10).
  • Truth and experience are connected. Feeling good about Jesus cannot be separated from whether He really is the Son of God, and hope for the future depends on whether the resurrection happened.
  • Competing worldviews demand clarity. Secular philosophies, religions, and cults compete for allegiance. A firm grasp of doctrine is a standard for measuring them, as studying genuine currency is how one learns to spot counterfeits (Erickson).

Where Theology Starts

Of the possible starting points—natural theology, tradition, Scripture, and experience—Erickson holds that the Bible is “the defining document or the constitution of the Christian faith.” As a constitution binds a government, the Bible defines correct belief and practice, and only its Author can change it.

A Method in Eight Steps

  1. Collect the biblical materials: identify and study every relevant passage.
  2. Unify what the biblical authors say on the topic.
  3. Analyze the meaning, without reading modern meanings into ancient texts.
  4. Examine how the church has treated the doctrine.
  5. Consult other cultural perspectives, so as not to mistake your own assumptions for Scripture’s teaching.
  6. Identify the doctrine’s essence: the permanent truth, apart from its cultural packaging.
  7. Draw on sources beyond the Bible, such as general revelation and other disciplines, as a supplement, never a substitute.
  8. Restate the doctrine for today’s audience (Erickson).

Four Working Principles

Etzel and Small add four principles: the Bible is the primary source; theology follows a method (gather data, test, analyze) that brings objectivity; the student engages every branch, because the branches build on one another; and theology should produce a practical outcome, with right belief (orthodoxy) leading to right living (orthopraxy) (Etzel and Small, 8–9). As Etzel and Gutierrez put it, “You will act upon what you deeply believe!” (Etzel and Gutierrez, x)

The Right Motivation

Jesus named the greatest commandment as loving God and loving others (Matt. 22:37–40), and Paul warns that knowledge without love is nothing (1 Cor. 13:2–3). The goal is not merely to know about God, but to know God, and to love Him and others more as a result (Etzel and Small, 10).

3 · Revelation
ReadEtzel and Gutierrez, Theology Applied
  • 6. Revelation pp. 31–35
Opens in Logos.

Because we are finite and God is infinite, we can know Him only if He takes the initiative to make Himself known (Erickson). We did not discover Him; even after the Fall, God sought Adam and Eve (Gen. 3:8–9).

General Revelation

General revelation is God’s communication of Himself to all people, at all times, in all places (Erickson). It comes in three modes:

  • Nature. “The heavens declare the glory of God” (Ps. 19:1). His “eternal power and divine nature, have been clearly perceived, ever since the creation of the world,” so that people “are without excuse” (Rom. 1:20).
  • History. God governs the nations and directs events (Acts 14:15–17), as in Israel’s preservation across the centuries.
  • Conscience. People sense that some things ought to be done whatever the cost to themselves; “the work of the law is written on their hearts” (Rom. 2:14–15).

General revelation is enough to show that God exists and to leave humanity without excuse, but it is not enough to save. Salvation requires knowing Jesus Christ (Rom. 10:9–10): “There is salvation in no one else” (Acts 4:12) (Etzel and Small, 16; Etzel and Gutierrez, 32). General revelation points people to their need for salvation; it does not supply it.

Special Revelation

Special revelation is God’s manifestation of Himself to particular people at definite times and places, so that they might enter a redemptive relationship with Him (Erickson). It became necessary because sin added a moral limit to the natural limit of our finitude (Erickson). It comes through historical events (the Exodus, the cross, the resurrection), divine speech (prophets, dreams, visions, and the written Word), and supremely the Incarnation: Jesus is the fullest revelation of God because He is God (John 1:14; Heb. 1:3; Col. 1:15). The Scriptures themselves bear witness to Him (John 5:39; Luke 24:27).

Psalm 19

Verses 1–6 show general revelation through creation; verses 7–11, special revelation through Scripture (“The law of the Lord is perfect, reviving the soul”); and verses 12–14, the psalmist’s response of conviction and trust in “my rock and my redeemer.” The two kinds of revelation do not compete. General revelation supplies the concepts that make special revelation understandable, and special revelation builds on it (Erickson).

4 · Inspiration and Inerrancy
ReadEtzel and Gutierrez, Theology Applied
  • 7. Inspiration/Inerrancy pp. 37–40
Opens in Logos.

Inspiration is the supernatural influence of the Holy Spirit on the writers of Scripture, so that what they wrote is the Word of God (Erickson). The Bible has two authors: human writers genuinely wrote it, and God directed the process so that the result is His Word (Etzel and Small, 28).

The Biblical Evidence

  • “All Scripture is breathed out by God” (2 Tim. 3:16). The Greek theopneustos means “God-breathed.”
  • “No prophecy was ever produced by the will of man, but men spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit” (2 Pet. 1:21).
  • Peter places Paul’s letters alongside “the other Scriptures” (2 Pet. 3:15–16).
  • Jesus treated the Old Testament as authoritative: “Scripture cannot be broken” (John 10:35), down to the smallest letter (Matt. 5:18).

Theories of Inspiration

TheoryDescriptionAssessment
IntuitionThe writers had a high natural religious insightReduces Scripture to human genius
IlluminationThe Spirit heightened the writers’ normal abilitiesDiffers only in degree from the Spirit’s work in every believer
DynamicThe Spirit directed the writers’ thoughts; their personalities chose the wordsValues dual authorship but may not reach the wording
VerbalThe Spirit’s influence extended to the choice of wordsAffirms the precision of the text; distinct from dictation
DictationGod dictated every word; the writer served as a secretaryCannot account for the distinct styles of the books

Four Qualities of Inspiration

  1. The Bible affirms both human and divine authorship.
  2. Inspiration incorporates the personality of the human author: Paul sounds like Paul; John sounds like John.
  3. The actual words of the Bible are the words God intended.
  4. The result is the inspired Word of God. Inspiration applies to the original documents (the autographs); translations are the Word of God insofar as they accurately represent them (Etzel and Small, 31).

Erickson adds that God prepared each writer over a lifetime of family, social, educational, and religious experience, so that the Spirit could direct their thoughts precisely without mechanical dictation. The whole Trinity was involved in that preparation.

Inerrancy

Inerrancy means the Bible, in its original manuscripts, is without error in all that it affirms. It flows from inspiration: if God, who is all-knowing and holy, inspired Scripture, His words are true (Etzel and Small, 32; Etzel and Gutierrez, 37). The Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy (1978), the most thorough modern treatment, has 29 articles of affirmation and denial. Article VI affirms “that the whole of Scripture and all its parts, down to the very words of the original, were given by divine inspiration.” Article VIII affirms that God “utilized the distinctive personalities and literary styles of the writers whom He had chosen and prepared,” and denies that He “overrode their personalities.”

Inerrancy matters on three levels (Etzel and Small, 32–33): theologically, because Jesus and the New Testament writers treated Scripture as authoritative and error-free; historically, because abandoning the Bible’s full trustworthiness has led to erosion in other doctrines; and epistemologically, because beliefs held on the Bible’s authority are only as trustworthy as the Bible is true.

5 · Interpretation and Illumination
ReadEtzel and Gutierrez, Theology Applied
  • 8. Interpretation pp. 43–46
  • 9. Illumination pp. 49–51
Opens in Logos.

Hermeneutics is the study of the principles for interpreting the biblical text, and of how author, text, and reader relate. Its first rule: “The Bible can never mean what it never meant” (Etzel and Gutierrez, 43). Begin with what the author intended to communicate to the original audience. A passage has one meaning and many applications.

Three Steps

  1. Observation. What does the text actually say? Look at the historical context, the author’s background, and the genre, before asking what it means to you.
  2. Interpretation. What is the text communicating? Paul urges Timothy to be “rightly handling the word of truth” (2 Tim. 2:15); the Greek orthotomeō means to “cut straight.”
  3. Application. How does the rightly interpreted truth bear on life today?

Practical Guidelines

  • Stand in their sandals. Ask how the first listeners heard the teaching before applying it today (Etzel and Gutierrez, 45).
  • Let Scripture interpret Scripture. Compare clear passages on a topic with less clear ones.
  • Use reliable tools. Concordances, commentaries, and background resources clarify context.

Illumination

Illumination is the Holy Spirit’s work in the reader of Scripture. It adds nothing to Scripture’s content and does not replace careful study; it enables believers to judge Scripture rightly, accept it, and obey it (1 Cor. 2:14). The psalmist prays, “Open my eyes, that I may behold wondrous things out of your law” (Ps. 119:18). Illumination is distinct from revelation, by which God makes truth known, and from inspiration, by which the Spirit superintended the writing.

  • Study and the Spirit work together. The Bereans were “examining the Scriptures daily to see if these things were so” (Acts 17:11) (Etzel and Small, 38).
  • Understanding grows. No one grasps everything in a passage the first time; understanding deepens over time as the believer draws closer to God (Etzel and Gutierrez, 50).
6 · Canon and Authority
ReadEtzel and Gutierrez, Theology Applied
  • 10. Canon pp. 53–56
Etzel and Small, Everyday Theology
  • 5. Can the Bible Be Trusted? pp. 35–39
Opens in Logos.

The word canon (Greek kanōn, “measuring rod” or “standard”) names both the Bible as the church’s standard of faith and practice and the list of inspired books: 39 in the Old Testament and 27 in the New, 66 in all.

The Old Testament

Written about 1400–400 BC, mostly in Hebrew with portions in Aramaic, and arranged as the Law, the Prophets, and the Writings. Jewish tradition holds that the Old Testament canon was complete by about 400 BC. The Dead Sea Scrolls (about 250 BC–AD 70) contain every canonical Old Testament book except Esther, and Jesus affirmed the Old Testament throughout His ministry (Matt. 5:17–18; Luke 24:44; John 5:39).

The New Testament

Written about AD 45–100, in Greek. Athanasius’s letter of AD 367 is the first known list of exactly our 27 books, and the councils of Hippo (393) and Carthage (397) formally recognized them (Etzel and Gutierrez, 53). The gap does not mean the church was unsure. Peter called Paul’s writings Scripture (2 Pet. 3:15–16), and Clement of Rome, Ignatius, and Polycarp cited New Testament writings as Scripture within a generation of the apostles (Etzel and Small, 24). The councils did not give these books authority; they recognized the authority the books already had from God (Etzel and Small, 26).

Three standards guided that recognition: apostolicity (written by an apostle or a close associate), the rule of faith (consistent with the clear teaching of the rest of Scripture), and consensus (widely used and accepted in the early churches).

Books in Question

  • The Apocrypha. Books written between the Testaments, most of which the Roman Catholic Church declared Scripture at the Council of Trent (1546). They are rejected because neither Jesus nor the apostles quoted them as authoritative, and they are not part of the Hebrew Scriptures.
  • The “lost gospels.” Writings such as the Gospel of Thomas and the Gospel of Judas, from the second century, bear apostles’ names but were written too late to be theirs. They fail the tests of apostolicity and the rule of faith.
  • Later additions. The Book of Mormon, published in the 1830s, meets none of the three standards.

Can the Bible Be Trusted?

Its trustworthiness rests on the preservation of the text and the work of the Holy Spirit (Etzel and Small, 36–38). Textual criticism compares manuscript copies to recover the original readings, and the thousands of copies, from fragments to whole books, agree remarkably. The Bible survived centuries of persecution and remains the most widely circulated book in history.

Translations fall along a spectrum: word-for-word (such as the KJV, NASB, and ESV), thought-for-thought (such as the NIV and NCV), and paraphrase (such as the Living Bible and The Message). Each can be considered Scripture insofar as it accurately represents the original manuscripts (Etzel and Small, 37).

A commitment to the Bible’s authority gives objectivity, a fixed point of reference in a world searching for truth; confidence that good modern translations faithfully render God’s Word; and, through the Spirit’s work, guidance in knowing and applying God’s will.

Deck 1: What Is Theology?

  1. What does prolegomena mean?

    Show answer

    Answer: Things said beforehand. From Greek pro, “before,” and legō, “to say”: the questions settled before the study begins.

  2. Theology draws together what the whole Bible says on a topic into one coherent whole. Which characteristic is this?

    Show answer

    Answer: Systematic. Systematic theology doesn’t study each book alone. It gathers the whole Bible’s teaching on a topic.

  3. Which branch of theology studies salvation?

    Show answer

    Answer: Soteriology. Soteriology is the study of salvation (Module 6). Eschatology covers last things, ecclesiology the church, and hamartiology sin.

  4. How does your view of sin bear on your view of salvation?

    Show answer

    Answer: Sin shows why salvation is needed. The branches connect: what you believe about sin shows what salvation must rescue us from.

Deck 2: Why and How We Study Theology

  1. One theologian compares knowing doctrine to studying genuine currency. What is the point?

    Show answer

    Answer: Knowing the true helps you spot the false. Many worldviews compete for allegiance. A firm grasp of doctrine is the standard for measuring them.

  2. The Bible has been called “the constitution of the Christian faith.” What does that mean?

    Show answer

    Answer: It defines what Christians believe and do, and only its Author can change it. As a constitution binds a government, Scripture defines correct belief and practice.

  3. A student reads a modern meaning into an ancient word. Which step of the method guards against this?

    Show answer

    Answer: Analyzing the meaning. Analysis asks what the words meant then and there, before restating the doctrine for today.

  4. A student plans to study only the branches of theology they find interesting. Which working principle does this break?

    Show answer

    Answer: Study every branch. The branches build on one another, so skipping one weakens the rest.

Deck 3: Revelation

  1. Which of these is a mode of general revelation?

    Show answer

    Answer: Human conscience. General revelation comes through nature, history, and conscience. The others are modes of special revelation.

  2. Someone sees God’s power in creation but has never heard of Christ. What does general revelation do for them?

    Show answer

    Answer: It leaves them without excuse, but it cannot save them. Creation shows God’s power, so people are “without excuse” (Rom. 1:20). But “there is salvation in no one else” (Acts 4:12).

  3. Why was special revelation needed?

    Show answer

    Answer: Sin added a moral limit to our natural limit as finite creatures. We were always finite. After the Fall we were also sinners, so fuller instruction became necessary.

  4. How do general and special revelation relate?

    Show answer

    Answer: General revelation lays the groundwork; special revelation builds on it. They share one subject and never conflict. General revelation points; special revelation saves.

Deck 4: Inspiration and Inerrancy

  1. In “All Scripture is breathed out by God” (2 Tim. 3:16), the Greek word is theopneustos. What does it mean?

    Show answer

    Answer: God-breathed. Scripture comes from God Himself. He breathed it out; He didn’t merely approve what people wrote.

  2. Which theory holds that the Spirit guided the writers’ thoughts, but not their words?

    Show answer

    Answer: Dynamic. The dynamic theory values dual authorship but may not reach the wording. Plenary verbal inspiration extends to the words themselves.

  3. In “plenary verbal inspiration,” what does plenary mean?

    Show answer

    Answer: All of Scripture. Plenary means full: every part of Scripture is inspired. Verbal means the inspiration extends to the very words.

  4. Strictly speaking, inerrancy applies to which text?

    Show answer

    Answer: The original manuscripts. Inspiration and inerrancy apply to the originals. A translation is God’s Word insofar as it renders them accurately.

Deck 5: Interpretation and Illumination

  1. “The Bible can never mean what it never meant.” What does this principle require you to do first?

    Show answer

    Answer: Find what the author meant for the first audience. A passage has one meaning and many applications. The meaning starts with what the author meant.

  2. Which order do the three steps follow?

    Show answer

    Answer: Observation, interpretation, application. First see what the text says, then what it means, and only then how it bears on your life.

  3. You come to an unclear passage. Which guideline helps most?

    Show answer

    Answer: Let Scripture interpret Scripture. Clear passages on the same topic shed light on less clear ones.

  4. How does illumination relate to careful study?

    Show answer

    Answer: Illumination works through diligent study; it doesn’t replace it. The Bereans examined “the Scriptures daily” (Acts 17:11). The Spirit works in the reader and adds nothing to Scripture.

Deck 6: Canon and Authority

  1. Did the early church councils give the New Testament books their authority?

    Show answer

    Answer: No; they recognized the authority the books already had from God. Peter already called Paul’s letters Scripture (2 Pet. 3:15–16). The councils recognized what the church had long received.

  2. A book bears an apostle’s name, but it was written in the second century. Which test does it fail?

    Show answer

    Answer: Apostolicity. The “lost gospels” were written too late to come from the apostles whose names they bear.

  3. Jesus spoke of “the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms” (Luke 24:44). What does this show?

    Show answer

    Answer: He affirmed the Old Testament as a whole. Jesus named the three parts of the Hebrew Scriptures, the Law, the Prophets, and the Writings, which open with the Psalms.

  4. How does a commitment to the Bible’s authority give objectivity?

    Show answer

    Answer: It provides a fixed point of reference outside ourselves. In a world searching for truth, Scripture is a standard that doesn’t shift with opinion.

Why This Matters

Theology and Life Are Inseparable

“Teaching theology is incomplete without also teaching how it can change someone’s life” (Etzel and Gutierrez, ix). Theology without application is like cocoa powder without water: the right ingredient, never the drink.

General Revelation Points; Special Revelation Saves

Knowing about God through nature, history, and conscience is not the same as knowing God. Only special revelation—Scripture, and ultimately Jesus Christ—gives the knowledge a saving relationship requires.

The Bible, a Guardrail

No one drives a mountain road to admire the guardrails, but when the fog rolls in and the road bends hard, they save lives. The completed canon marks the edges of the road for every life.
